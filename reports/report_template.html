The method in four lines

  1. Pressure = leverage. A win-probability model built on this data tells us how far one ball can swing the match. The top {{p.pressure_top|.0f}}% of balls by that swing in each innings are pressure balls; a first-innings pressure ball counts {{lev.stake_w1|.2f}} of a chase one, in proportion to what is at stake.
  2. Performance = Impact. The win probability a player adds on a ball, divided by what was at stake, compared with the league average in the same situation.
  3. Rating = level, shift and consistency. {{p.w_level|.0f}}% pressure level, {{p.w_shift|.0f}}% change from the player's usual level, {{p.w_cons|.0f}}% share of pressure matches in which he added win probability, after shrinking small samples towards the average.
  4. Checks. Rank intervals from {{p.n_boot|int}} match resamples, a 2022–24 → 2025–26 time split, and {{p.n_variants|int}} alternative versions of the method.

Three findings

  1. The usual definition of pressure measures the wrong balls. "Death overs, or a required rate above 10" misses {{naive.chase_missed|.0f}}% of the high-leverage balls in chases, and {{naive.chase_usual_dead_below|.0f}}% of the chase balls it does flag are dead or below-median leverage.
  2. Pressure balls really are different, but not everywhere. Comparing like with like (same over, same wickets down), chasing batters score {{val.w_2_all_SR_value|abs.0f}} strike-rate points slower on pressure balls ({{val.w_2_middle_SR_value|abs.0f}} in the middle overs), while first-innings batters score faster. In the first innings, "pressure" is mostly the new ball and carries about half the stake, so it counts about half, and each pick's chase-only rank is shown too.
  3. Under pressure, level matters more than nerve. Players do differ in how their run-scoring and run-saving hold up under pressure (not in wickets), but the differences are small next to chance and barely carry over: rated on 2022–24, a bowler's usual level is the best guide to his 2025–26 pressure form ({{ts.bowl_ulevel|.2f}}), and no measure we tried predicts batters' later pressure form clearly, so the batting list records who has delivered (5.2).

What we stand behind. {{sb.names90}} are top 10 in at least 90% of {{p.n_boot|int}} match resamples. {{sens.bat_core|int}} batters and {{sens.bowl_core|int}} bowlers are top 10 under at least {{p.core_min|int}} of {{p.n_variants|int}} versions of the method. {{sb.bat_allchecks_names}} pass every check (secure or probable, top 10 when chasing, still top 10 without their best match). {{sb.bat_ipl_top10|int}} of the 10 batters and {{sb.bowl_ipl_top10|int}} of the 10 bowlers are also top 10 on IPL balls alone; the others lean on T20I evidence, flagged (⚑) where it is thin. Less sure: {{conf.bat_marginal|int}} batters and {{conf.bowl_marginal|int}} bowlers are marginal, and {{sb.bat_drop_names}} (batting) and {{sb.bowl_drop_names}} (bowling) drop out of the top 10 without their best pressure match.

1.1 A win-probability model built on this data

Pressure starts from win probability (WP): the chance that the batting side wins, given the score, balls left, wickets in hand and, in a chase, the target. We fit it on {{wp.train_balls|int}} balls from {{wp.train_matches|int}} completed matches. Associate T20Is, DLS-affected games, ties and no-results are left out of fitting (their outcomes are mismatches or unreliable) but are still scored.

  • First innings: a logistic regression on how far the projected total sits above par. Par is the average first-innings total in the same competition tier and season, leaving the match itself out, which tracks the rise in IPL scoring year by year.
  • Chase: a logistic regression on how many runs the chasing side is ahead of an average side with the same balls and wickets left.
  • Last {{p.late_b|int}} balls of a chase: the regression misjudges the final over, so WP is computed exactly by playing out every remaining ball with league outcome rates for the same runs-per-ball requirement, fully so on the last ball.

The two regressions, as fitted. σ is the logistic function; b = balls left, w = wickets in hand, r = runs needed. E(b, w) is the expected remaining runs of an average side, a Poisson regression on b and w, made never to fall as wickets in hand rise; f = par ÷ E(120, 10) = par ÷ {{wp.erem120|.0f}} scales it to the competition and season.

First innings: WP = σ({{wp.f0|+.3f}} {{wp.f1|+.3f}}·z {{wp.f2|+.3f}}·z·p {{wp.f3|+.3f}}·z·p²),
z = (score + f·E(b, w) − par′) ÷ 30, p = 1 − b ÷ B

Chase: WP = σ({{wp.c0|+.3f}} {{wp.c1|+.3f}}·u {{wp.c2|+.3f}}·u·ln(b+1) {{wp.c3|+.3f}}·m/30 {{wp.c4|+.3f}}·w/10 {{wp.c5|+.3f}}·b/120 {{wp.c6|+.3f}}·RRR/10),
m = f·E(b, w) − r (runs ahead of an average side), u = m ÷ √(b+1)

B = balls in the innings; par′ = par scaled to B for a shortened innings; RRR = required run rate, capped at 36. In the last six balls of a chase the exact play-out is blended in with weight (7 − b)/6. Fitted with scikit-learn on the training matches by src/02_wp_model.py.

{{table:wp}}

Brier = mean squared error of the predicted probability (lower is better; the constant guess predicts the average win rate). Calibration error = average gap between predicted and actual win rates over ten probability bins, on matches the model never saw ({{p.folds|int}}-fold cross-validation by match).

Sanity checks (chasing side with 5 wickets in hand): 6 needed off the last ball, WP {{wp.6_off_1|.2f}}; 4 off the last ball {{wp.4_off_1|.2f}}; 12 off 3 {{wp.12_off_3|.2f}}; 6 off 6 {{wp.6_off_6|.2f}}; 30 off 6 {{wp.30_off_6|.2f}}.

The chase model is strong (AUC {{wp.auc_2|.2f}}). The first-innings model is weaker (AUC {{wp.auc_1|.2f}}): before the chase starts, much of the result is still unknown. First-innings leverage inherits that, which is one reason we report a chase-only rank for every pick.

1.2 Leverage: how much one ball can swing the match

For every ball we compute the WP after each of {{p.n_outcomes|int}} possible outcomes (dot, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, wicket, extra), weight them by how often each happens in that innings and phase, and take their spread (standard deviation). This spread, in WP points, is the ball's leverage; Impact (2.1) divides by it. Dividing it by the all-ball average gives the Leverage Index (LI), used to compare balls: an average ball is 1.00; a ball with LI 3 carries three times the usual stake. The 95th percentile is {{lev.p95_li|.2f}}, the 99th {{lev.p99_li|.2f}}, and the highest, the last ball of a close chase, {{lev.max_li|.1f}}.

1.3 Pressure balls

A pressure ball is one whose LI is at or above the {{p.pressure_pct|ord}} percentile of its own innings, so {{p.pressure_top|.0f}}% of the balls in each innings qualify ({{lev.pressure_balls|int}} in all). A dead ball cannot move WP by more than {{p.dead_pts|.1f}} points; these ({{lev.dead_pct|.1f}}% of balls, mostly lost causes) are dropped, so runs in a dead game count for nothing. Everything else is a routine ball.

Why the cut is set within each innings. Chase leverage is either near zero or very high, while first-innings leverage is flat. One league-wide cut would make {{lev.global_cut_chase_pct|.0f}}% of pressure balls chase balls, and nobody would be rated on setting a total. So pressure is measured relative to the job, and we show the split openly.

{{table:pressure_profile}}

How much each innings counts. The two kinds of pressure ball do not carry the same stake: the median pressure ball has LI {{lev.median_li_1|.2f}} in the first innings and {{lev.median_li_2|.2f}} in a chase. So in the rating a first-innings pressure ball counts {{lev.stake_w1|.2f}} of a chase pressure ball (the ratio of the two medians). First-innings play is still rated, but the new ball cannot outweigh a tight chase. The ratio is measured, not chosen, and was fixed before any re-ranking; equal weights are variant V13 (section 5.3).

What first-innings pressure is. It is mostly the new ball: {{lev.over1_flag|.0f}}% of first-over balls and {{lev.over2_flag|.0f}}% of second-over balls are pressure balls, and first-innings powerplay balls are {{lev.inn1_pp_share_all|.0f}}% of all pressure balls. Early wickets move the projected total most, so that is where the first-innings stakes are, but they are only mildly high (median LI {{lev.median_li_1|.2f}} against {{lev.median_li_2|.2f}} in chases). Chase pressure sits in close games: {{lev.chase_close_pct|.0f}}% of chase pressure balls come with WP between 20% and 80%.

Which picks this affects most. Among the top 10s, {{fi.names}} have at least 75% of their pressure balls in the first innings; their chase-only ranks in Tables 1–2 show who also holds up when chasing.

Match importance. Knockouts and finals are not part of the pressure definition. LI measures how far one ball can swing this match; a knockout raises the cost of losing the match, not the swing of a ball. The data agrees: balls in the {{ko.matches|int}} knockout matches with ball-by-ball data ({{ko.ipl|int}} IPL playoffs, {{ko.t20i|int}} full-member T20Is, {{ko.emerging|int}} emerging-team games) have an average LI of {{ko.mean_li|.2f}} against {{ko.mean_li_other|.2f}} elsewhere, and {{ko.pressure_pct|.0f}}% of them are pressure balls against {{ko.pressure_pct_other|.0f}}%. So a tight knockout finish is already rated as pressure, ball by ball, and an easy knockout win is not. Knockout samples per player are too small to rank on (the batting picks have between {{ko.bat_picks_min_balls|int}} and {{ko.bat_picks_max_balls|int}} knockout pressure balls each, and only {{ko.bowl_picks_with|int}} of the 10 bowling picks have any); section 4 reports their knockout record pooled, as a check.

First-innings pressure balls are defined by their stakes (an early wicket moves the projected total most), not by a change in how batters play, which is one reason they count {{lev.stake_w1|.2f}} of a chase pressure ball (1.3). The rating rewards performance on high-stakes balls; whether players also differ in how they respond is tested, not assumed: batters do; bowlers do in run-saving but not in wicket-taking (3.1).

The usual definition misses most pressure balls. In chases, "overs 16–20 or required rate above 10" misses {{naive.chase_missed|.0f}}% of our pressure balls and {{naive.crunch_missed|.0f}}% of crunch balls (top {{p.crunch_pct|.0f}}% of LI), while {{naive.chase_usual_dead_below|.0f}}% of the balls it flags are dead or below-median leverage.

2. Performance measurement Brief item: batting and bowling metrics, baselines, rating logic

2.1 Value of one ball: Impact

Impact = (WP after the ball − WP before) ÷ leverage of the ball (in WP points) − league average for the same situation

Impact is how much a ball helped the team's win chances, measured against how much was at stake on it, compared with what an average player does in the same situation. Raw WP added is larger on pressure balls simply because more is at stake; dividing by leverage puts pressure and routine balls on one scale, so the two can be compared. Impact is split into a run part (the WP change without the wicket) and a wicket part, each capped at ±{{p.x_clip|.0f}} so one freak ball cannot dominate. Figures are per 100 balls; positive helps the player's team.

  • Batter: the run part on every ball faced, and the wicket part only when he is out. Wides are not the striker's; a non-striker run-out is charged to nobody.
  • Bowler: every delivery, wides and no-balls included, with the sign flipped; the wicket part only for bowler wickets (not run-outs).
  • Centring: from each ball we subtract the league-average credited Impact in the same innings, over group (1–2, 3–6, 7–10, 11–15, 16–18, 19–20) and pressure flag. Without this, openers looked worse and new-ball bowlers better under pressure purely because of where they play: eligible batters' average shift was {{cent.bat_before|+.1f}} per 100 balls before centring and {{cent.bat_after|+.1f}} after; bowlers' {{cent.bowl_before|+.1f}} and {{cent.bowl_after|+.1f}}.

2.4 The rating

Rating = {{p.w_level|.0f}}% · z(pressure level) + {{p.w_shift|.0f}}% · z(shift) + {{p.w_cons|.0f}}% · z(consistency),   reported as 50 + 10 × rating

Each z-score divides the player's shrunk value by the spread of the raw values across the pool. {{table:effective}}

Why level leads. Pressure level is what wins matches and is the best-measured component. The shift is the brief's "compared with their usual level", but it is the difference of two noisy numbers, so for bowlers it is shrunk almost to zero (shrunk shifts range {{pool.bowl_shift_min|+.1f}} to {{pool.bowl_shift_max|+.1f}} per 100 balls, against {{pool.bat_shift_min|+.1f}} to {{pool.bat_shift_max|+.1f}} for batters). Never the drop alone: a player who is poor in every situation has no drop at all, so ranking on the drop would reward him. Why bowlers' consistency sits below 50%: two-thirds of a bowler's pressure spells bring no wicket, and without one his match WPA is usually slightly negative; wickets are rare but large. So the average eligible bowler has positive pressure WPA in {{cons.bowl_pool|.0f}}% of pressure matches and the average batter in {{cons.bat_pool|.0f}}%. Read each player against his own list's average.

3. Fair comparisons Brief item: sample sizes, eligibility, match context, IPL + T20I

3.1 Shrinking small samples (empirical Bayes)

Each estimate is pulled towards the pool average by an amount set by the data: shrunk = average + n ÷ (n + k) × (raw − average), where n is the player's effective number of balls. k is the number of balls at which we trust the player's own record as much as the league average, estimated from how much players really differ compared with ball-to-ball noise. Run and wicket parts are shrunk separately because wickets take far longer to settle. Priors and k are estimated on players with at least {{p.eb_total|int}} balls and {{p.eb_pressure|int}} pressure balls.

{{table:eb}}

"Cap" = the data shows almost no real spread between bowlers' overall shifts or their wicket-part shifts, so k hits the {{p.k_cap|int}}-ball ceiling and the shift is close to the average for everyone.

What this means for the bowling list. Over a typical bowler's sample ({{eb.bowl_nP_median|.0f}} pressure balls once recency and competition weights are applied), a bowler's raw pressure Impact moves by about ±{{eb.bowl_P_se_median|.1f}} per 100 balls through chance alone, and the gaps between bowlers' pressure and usual Impact, taken as a whole, are no wider than chance would produce. So the best estimate of a bowler's pressure level is close to his usual level, and the bowling list ranks bowlers on high-stakes balls largely through their overall level. Table 2 shows each pick's raw pressure Impact with its chance range. {{bowl1.player_name}} is the sharpest case: {{bowl1.P_raw|+.1f}} ± {{bowl1.P_raw_se|.1f}} on pressure balls against {{bowl1.U|+.1f}} usual, a gap of {{bowl1.P_gap_se|abs.1f}} chance ranges; among {{pool.bowl|int}} bowlers, chance alone produces about {{eb.bowl1_expected_gaps|.0f}} gaps that large in either direction. For batters the gaps are wider than chance, so their shift counts (2.4).

Split into runs and wickets. The run-saving gap varies between bowlers {{eb.bowl_D_x_run_ratio|.2f}} times as much as chance would produce (k = {{eb.bowl_D_x_run_k|.0f}} balls); the wicket gap varies {{eb.bowl_D_x_wkt_ratio|.2f}} times as much, i.e. not beyond chance (batters: {{eb.bat_D_x_run_ratio|.2f}} and {{eb.bat_D_x_wkt_ratio|.2f}}). So bowlers do differ in how many runs they give away under pressure, not in how often they take wickets, and in the total the wicket noise hides the run signal. The run difference is real but small next to chance. Counting it separately (variant V14) reorders the bowling list ({{sens.bowl_v14_overlap|int}} of 10 kept; {{bowl1.player_name}} {{sens.bowl1_v14_rank|ord}}) without predicting 2025–26 better ({{ts.bowl_v14|.2f}} against {{ts.bowl_rating|.2f}}, within noise, 5.2), and it makes the list less stable. Under V14, {{v14.bowl_secure|int}} bowlers would be secure, {{v14.bowl_probable|int}} probable and {{v14.bowl_marginal|int}} marginal (headline: {{conf.bowl_secure|int}}, {{conf.bowl_probable|int}} and {{conf.bowl_marginal|int}}); {{v14.bowl_stay|int}} of 10 would stay top 10 without their best match (headline {{mc.bowl_stay|int}}); and {{v14.bowl_ipl_top10|int}} would be top 10 on IPL balls alone (headline {{sb.bowl_ipl_top10|int}}). For batters it changes little ({{v14.bat_secure|int}} secure, {{v14.bat_marginal|int}} marginal; {{v14.bat_stay|int}} survive losing their best match). So the headline keeps one shift, fixed before any ranking was seen, and Tables 1–2 give every pick's rank under V14.

5. Reliability and limitations Brief item: consistency across matches, uncertainty

5.1 How sure is each place?

We resample whole matches {{p.n_boot|int}} times and re-rank. {{conf.bat_secure|int}} batters and {{conf.bowl_secure|int}} bowlers stay in the top 10 in at least {{p.secure_pct|.0f}}% of resamples. The order inside a confidence band is the point estimate only, and places 8–12 cannot be told apart: #11 {{boot.bat_name_11}} is top 10 in {{boot.bat_share_11|.0f}}% of resamples and #11 {{boot.bowl_name_11}} in {{boot.bowl_share_11|.0f}}%. The intervals hold the WP model and shrinkage settings fixed, so the true uncertainty is larger.

Does each pick deliver match after match?

The brief asks for the players who deliver most consistently, so we test every pick against his own record. Rank without best match: his rank with his single best pressure match removed (by pressure Impact), everyone else unchanged and the shrinkage settings held fixed; a one-match wonder drops out. Positive-Impact matches: the share of his pressure matches (at least {{p.match_min_balls|int}} pressure balls) in which his Impact was positive, against the eligible average of {{mc.bat_pool_pct|.0f}}% for batters and {{mc.bowl_pool_pct|.0f}}% for bowlers. This differs from the consistency component in the rating, which counts matches in which he added win probability, an outcome view that the biggest balls dominate; the share here uses Impact, the situation-adjusted measure used everywhere else, so a reader can check that the picks hold up either way. Best match's share: how much of his total pressure Impact came from that one match (– where the total is not positive). 5.2 Does pressure form repeat?

We rate players on 2022–24 only and check how well each measure predicts their raw pressure Impact in 2025–26 (players with at least {{p.time_min_pressure|int}} pressure balls in both periods). Because the 2025–26 outcome is itself noisy, no predictor could exceed about {{ts.bat_ceiling|.2f}} for batters and {{ts.bowl_ceiling|.2f}} for bowlers (split-half reliability). For bowlers, pressure level ({{ts.bowl_level|.2f}}, 90% interval {{ts.bowl_level_lo|+.2f}} to {{ts.bowl_level_hi|+.2f}}) recovers a fair part of what is recoverable; the full rating ({{ts.bowl_rating|.2f}}, {{ts.bowl_rating_lo|+.2f}} to {{ts.bowl_rating_hi|+.2f}}) less clearly. The best single guide is the plain usual level ({{ts.bowl_ulevel|.2f}}): a bowler who is good in routine overs is good under pressure. For batters, the rating shows no persistence ({{ts.bat_rating|+.2f}}, 90% interval {{ts.bat_rating_lo|+.2f}} to {{ts.bat_rating_hi|+.2f}}); raw pressure Impact ({{ts.bat_raw|.2f}}) and the usual death-overs strike rate ({{ts.bat_usual|.2f}}) do slightly better, but their intervals overlap zero and each other.

Is that a by-product of our design choices? No. The full ratings of five of the variants in 5.3, rated the same way on 2022–24, give the same picture: for batters, none clears zero (chases only (V3) {{ts.bat_v3|+.2f}}, pressure level only (V4) {{ts.bat_v4|+.2f}}, raw WP added (V12) {{ts.bat_v12|+.2f}}, both innings weighted equally (V13) {{ts.bat_v13|+.2f}}, shift split into runs and wickets (V14) {{ts.bat_v14|+.2f}}); for bowlers {{ts.bowl_v3|+.2f}}, {{ts.bowl_v4|+.2f}}, {{ts.bowl_v12|+.2f}}, {{ts.bowl_v13|+.2f}} and {{ts.bowl_v14|+.2f}}. The only batting measure whose interval clears zero is the raw shift ({{ts.bat_shift|+.2f}}, {{ts.bat_shift_lo|+.2f}} to {{ts.bat_shift_hi|+.2f}}), and only just, as one of eleven tried on about {{ts.bat_n|int}} players, so we do not build on it. We did not retune the weights. The batting list is the best evidence of who has delivered, not a forecast.

Why keep the rating if a simpler measure predicts as well? The brief asks who has delivered under pressure, a description; the time split tests a forecast. The rating is built to describe delivery fairly (stakes, situation, sample size, competition). Choosing whichever of eleven predictors happens to score best on about {{ts.bat_n|int}} players would be choosing on noise, since their intervals overlap. What the time split does settle is how to read the lists: the bowling list carries over better than the batting list.

5.4 Limitations

  • Batting persistence. The batter rating does not predict the next period (5.2). Treat batting places beyond the secure ones as a record of delivery.
  • Small samples. {{bat1.player_name}}'s evidence is almost all from one season; {{bat3.player_name}} is close to the career-volume minimum ({{bat3.elig_balls|int}} balls). Both are flagged (⚑) in Table 1.
  • IPL evidence. {{bowl9.player_name}} has {{bowl9.ipl_pressure_balls|int}} IPL pressure balls, {{bowl4.player_name}} {{bowl4.ipl_pressure_balls|int}} and {{bat6.player_name}} {{bat6.ipl_pressure_balls|int}}, so all three are flagged (⚑); their ratings rest mainly on T20Is, adjusted by an offset measured on the players who play in both (Table in 3.3) and down-weighted.
  • First-innings pressure is mostly the new ball, and the first-innings WP model is weaker (AUC {{wp.auc_1|.2f}}). The chase-only rank in section 4 shows who also holds up when chasing.
  • Last ball of a chase. After the exact play-out, the model is still slightly pessimistic there on average ({{wp.last_ball_dwp|+.1f}} WP points per ball, standard error {{wp.last_ball_se|.1f}}, {{wp.last_ball_n|int}} balls).
  • Role effects. Centring removes most of the effect of where a player bats; the correlation between batters' role mix and their shift falls from {{cent.bat_corr_before|.2f}} to {{cent.bat_corr_after|.2f}}, not to zero.
  • No venue or opposition adjustment. Venue names are missing (anonymous ids only), and opposition strength is only handled through competition tiers. A descriptive check in 3.3 shows where it may matter: {{opp.max_name}} has {{opp.max_pct|.0f}}% of his full-member T20I pressure balls against the two weakest full members (eligible average {{opp.bowl_pool_pct|.0f}}%).
  • DLS and short games. {{d.dls_no_target|int}} DLS games have no revised target, so their chases are not scored; DLS games are left out of fitting; other revised targets are taken from the data.
  • Accounting simplifications. Byes and leg-byes sit inside the run part for both batter and bowler. Super overs are excluded; {{d.tied|int}} tied matches are scored with a level finish worth 0.5.
